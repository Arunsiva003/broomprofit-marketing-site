Why a booked cleaning job can still lose money

Revenue is what the customer pays. Profit is what's left after the costs of providing the service.

First, identify the job costs

A useful estimate includes loaded labor cost, supplies, mileage and paid travel, parking, and a rational allocation of overhead. Card processing may be a percentage of the charge plus a fixed amount. Numbers in this article are examples only—not suggested prices or wage benchmarks.

An illustrative quote

Imagine $180 in labor, $18 in supplies, $10 in travel expenses, and $12 in allocated overhead. Base job costs are $220. With a 3% processing fee and a 25% target job margin, the simple price floor is $220 / (1 − 0.03 − 0.25) = $305.56. Rounding the price to $310 leaves approximately $80.70 after the modeled costs and processing percentage, or 26.0% of revenue.

What changes after the job?

If labor actually costs $240 instead of $180, the same $310 customer price leaves approximately $20.70, a 6.7% modeled margin. The job was booked, but the estimate missed an important expense.

Make the analysis a habit

After service, record the real paid person-hours and materials, actual travel, and amount billed. Review differences by cleaning type across multiple jobs. This helps identify repeated underestimating instead of relying on intuition or generic per-square-foot rates.
