Person-hours vs. clock-hours: the cleaning estimate trap

The hours your customer sees are not always the same as the paid labor-hours your business must cover.

What is a person-hour?

One person working for one hour creates one person-hour of labor. Two cleaners each working for three hours create six person-hours, while the customer sees a three-hour visit. This distinction matters whenever the crew is paid by time.

Example: a two-person crew

Suppose a job requires six person-hours of cleaning and can be divided efficiently between two people. The estimated on-site elapsed time is approximately three hours. At an illustrative fully loaded labor cost of $22 per person-hour, on-site labor would cost $132.

If both workers are paid for an extra 30 minutes of travel or setup, the job adds one more person-hour, increasing modeled paid labor to seven person-hours. That alone changes labor cost to $154 before supplies, parking, overhead, and fees.

Three mistakes to avoid

  • Multiplying elapsed crew hours by just one worker's hourly cost.
  • Assuming two workers halve total labor cost. They may shorten the appointment, but both must be paid.
  • Forgetting paid drive time or setup time under applicable pay policies.

Track actual paid hours

Real data matters. A job that repeatedly takes longer than planned may need more scope clarification, changed production assumptions, or a larger buffer. Don't raise or lower estimates from one unusual job without understanding the cause.
